Founder Note

Butterscotch Bakes

Thoughts on getting from ₹2L MRR → ₹7L MRR while building a business that can eventually become a memorable consumer brand.

The goal isn't simply to increase revenue.

It's to discover a growth engine that gets to ₹7L MRR today, ₹20L tomorrow, and can eventually support a much larger brand.

Ground truth — what we actually know

  • Current monthly revenue is approximately ₹2L MRR.
  • The immediate milestone is reaching ₹7L MRR.
  • You ultimately want to build a consumer brand, not just another bakery.
  • The biggest open question is whether the least-resistant path to ₹7L comes from B2B, B2C, or a combination.
  • The brand itself still needs clarity around positioning, customer, pricing and identity.

Everything below is a framework for making those decisions — not an assumption that the answers already exist.

The first decision

Before discussing logos, interiors or expansion, answer one question:

What business are we actually trying to build?

OptionWhat success looks likeTrade-off
B2B-firstPredictable recurring revenue through cafés, offices, hotels and other businesses.Fast growth, but the customer may never know your brand.
B2C-firstA memorable neighbourhood bakery people actively seek out.Higher marketing, rent and execution risk.
HybridB2B funds the business while B2C builds the brand.Requires balancing two very different operating models.

A useful example

One reason a business like Suchali's Artisan Bakehouse became interesting is that it didn't try to win everything at once.

By supplying premium baked goods to cafés such as Blue Tokai, it built production capability and recurring revenue before aggressively expanding its own consumer retail.

The lesson isn't to copy that exact path.

The cheapest path to building a brand isn't always opening more customer-facing stores.

Sometimes another channel quietly funds the dream.

The Growth Model Example tab shows what that could look like, concretely, for Butterscotch.

Brand questions worth answering

Instead of starting with colours or interiors, define the business first.

  • Who is the ideal customer, and what occasion are they buying for — morning ritual, coffee break, celebration, takeaway?
  • What should people remember Butterscotch for?
  • If customers could describe the brand in one sentence, what should that sentence be?
  • Why should someone choose Butterscotch over another boutique bakery?
  • What can Butterscotch become genuinely famous for over the next five years?

Questions before spending more capital

  • Which channel currently generates the healthiest economics?
  • Where does repeat business naturally come from, and which products drive it?
  • What part of today's business is genuinely differentiated?
  • What capabilities already exist that competitors would find hard to replicate?
  • If ₹20L were available tomorrow, where would deploying it create the highest long-term return?

Recommended next steps

1. Get clarity before committing capital

Before large investments into more consumer retail, validate where growth naturally wants to come from. An experienced F&B consultant with expertise in menu engineering, unit economics and retail strategy can prevent far larger mistakes later.

2. Understand where ₹7L actually comes from

Instead of treating ₹7L as one target, break it into possible engines: additional B2B customers, higher revenue from existing customers, new consumer acquisition, better repeat purchases, higher average order value. The answer may be one of these — or a combination.

3. Define the brand before scaling it

Growth amplifies whatever already exists. A clear position today is far easier than repositioning five stores later.

4. Delay irreversible decisions

Large leases, expensive interiors and rapid expansion should ideally come only after there's confidence in both the business model and the brand direction.

Direction for the next 90 days

  1. Understand exactly where growth can come from fastest.
  2. Choose whether B2B, B2C or Hybrid becomes the primary engine.
  3. Clearly define the brand's customer, positioning and promise.
  4. Validate the model with measurable results before increasing fixed costs.
  5. Only then begin investing heavily into consumer brand building.

Final thought

The question isn't "How do we reach ₹7L MRR?"

The better question is "What engine gets us to ₹7L, then ₹20L, then ₹1Cr/month without reinventing the business every time?"

Once that engine is identified, everything else — brand, stores, marketing, expansion — becomes much easier to decide.

How to read this note

Every rupee figure here is a benchmark range for a Bangalore bakery in the Indiranagar–Domlur–HAL belt, not a claim about your books. I've flagged the ten numbers I'd need to replace them at the end. Where I'm guessing, I say so.

One assumption I've carried through from your brief: the ₹2L is revenue, most of it B2B, and the three shops are small counters rather than seated cafés. If any of that is wrong, Scenario D changes shape — tell me and I'll redo the bridge.

The diagnosis: retail is a cost centre pretending to be a business

Three counters at ~₹67,000 a month each cannot cover their own rent, staff and power in that part of Bangalore. So one of two things is true. Either B2B is quietly subsidising three shopfronts, or all four lines are underwater and the runway is personal savings. Both are fixable this quarter; neither is fixed by "doing more marketing".

Why "get to ₹7L" is the right target and the wrong first question

₹7L a month at a 50% blended gross margin gives you ~₹3.5L to cover a central kitchen, a flagship, a small team and yourself. That's the first level where the business pays its own salaries and you stop being the working capital. So the target is sound. But ₹7L of the wrong revenue — heavily discounted delivery, or one wholesale account at 22% margin — leaves you exactly as fragile at ₹7L as you are at ₹2L, with more staff to pay.

Optimise for gross profit per unit of your attention, not top line. That single reframe changes which of the scenarios below you pick.

The four engines you can actually pull

There are only four ways revenue enters a bakery like yours. They have very different margins, cash cycles and brand consequences — and founders usually pick the one that feels busiest rather than the one that pays.

The one most bakeries miss

Frozen laminated dough. You are already running lamination for your own puffs — sheeting, folding, resting, proofing. Every specialty café in Bangalore wants a croissant and a puff on the counter, and almost none of them wants to hire a pastry chef, buy a sheeter, and manage butter at 26°C. Today they either import frozen French dough at a painful landed cost or serve something mediocre from a local supplier.

Selling blast-frozen, par-baked or raw laminated dough to those cafés is higher margin than fresh wholesale, has a 90-day shelf life instead of 12 hours, kills your wastage problem, and makes you the picks-and-shovels supplier to a category that keeps growing whether or not any single café survives. It is also the only line here that a strategic acquirer or a VC will call a real asset in year seven.

Four scenarios, with the arithmetic

Each of these gets you to ₹7L. They cost very different amounts of money, time and optionality. I've put my recommendation last.

A. The wholesale sprint

Go all-in on supplying cafés, cloud kitchens and office pantries. Treat retail as a showroom you barely staff.

Time to ₹7L4–6 months
Capex₹6–12L
Blended GM~35%
RiskConcentration

Twenty-two accounts at an average ₹25,000 a month gets you ₹5.5L of new revenue. That is roughly one new account closed every four working days — hard but ordinary for a founder-led sales push in a city this dense with cafés.

Why it can work

  • Fastest path. No new site, no interiors, no landlord.
  • Near-zero customer acquisition cost — it's you, a sample box and a scooter.
  • Production planning gets easy: you bake to confirmed orders, wastage collapses.
  • Uses capacity you're already paying for at 2am.

What it costs you

  • 35% margin means ₹7L only throws off ₹2.45L of gross profit. Thin.
  • 30–60 day payment terms — growth eats cash, and you fund the butter.
  • If three accounts are 40% of revenue, they own your pricing.
  • You build no consumer brand. Goal 2 stalls for a year.

B. The flagship café

Build one proper 500–700 sq ft café with serious coffee. Make it the thing people photograph.

Time to ₹7L9–15 months
Capex₹25–45L
Blended GM~68%
RiskSingle-site bet

A good Indiranagar café does 90–130 bills a day at a ₹280–350 average. At 110 bills × ₹310, that's ₹34,000 a day — ₹10L a month from one address. The upside is real. So is the failure rate: a third of new cafés in that belt don't see year three, and you'd be putting the entire company on one lease.

Why it can work

  • Coffee is the margin engine: 78–85% gross on every cup, and it's what makes people come four times a week instead of once.
  • It's the only asset that builds goal 2 — the consumer brand — directly.
  • Cash the same evening. No receivables.
  • A working flagship is the unit you'd later replicate or franchise.

What it costs you

  • ₹25–45L you probably don't have, plus 10 months' deposit locked up.
  • Nine to fifteen months before it matters. Your ₹2L problem is now.
  • Wrong street, wrong side of the street, wrong frontage — and the money is gone.
  • Demands your full attention at exactly the moment wholesale needs selling.

C. The channel blitz

List everything on Swiggy and Zomato, push into quick commerce with packaged bakes, buy visibility.

Time to ₹7L3–5 months
Capex₹2–5L
Blended GM~22% net
RiskMargin collapse

The fastest top-line number on this page, and the most dangerous. Commission of 22–30%, ads at 5–8%, discounts at 10–20%: a ₹60 puff nets you about ₹33 before you've bought the butter. You can hit ₹7L of "revenue" here and lose money doing it.

Why it can work

  • Genuine discovery in a 4km radius you can't reach any other way.
  • Ratings and review volume become real proof for the brand.
  • Works honestly on ₹450+ baskets — croissant boxes, cakes, brownie slabs — where the fixed platform cut is diluted.

What it costs you

  • Laminated pastry travels badly. A soggy croissant at 40 minutes is a one-star review of your core product.
  • You train customers to only buy at 40% off, forever.
  • Q-commerce needs 5–7 day shelf-life SKUs — cookies, rusks, tea cakes — which is a different business from lamination.
  • Platform economics change without your consent.

Wholesale pays the bills and funds the kitchen. One flagship builds the brand. Nothing in the middle.

Why this is the pick

  • Wholesale cash flow funds the flagship, so you don't need outside money to start.
  • The same lamination line feeds both ends — one kitchen, one skill, two customers.
  • Every engine is a hedge on the others. Losing one account doesn't end you.
  • In year 7, you own two sellable things: a consumer brand and a supply business.

What you're trading away

  • It is slower to ₹7L than pure wholesale by 2–3 months.
  • Two businesses means two operating rhythms — you cannot run both. Hire the B2B seller or the café manager by month 3.
  • Blended 50% margin is worse than a pure café's 68%. You're buying resilience with margin.
  • Closing two shops means writing off deposits and having a hard conversation with staff.

The brand: own the puff, and let coffee do the maths

You've named the two things you want to be known for. One of them is a crowded fight and one is an open goal, and I don't think they're the ones you'd guess.

The wedge

The croissant war in Bangalore is already loud, expensive and won on Instagram by people with better funding. The puff is different. Every Indian adult has eaten a hundred of them — at the Iyengar bakery, at the darshini, from a warm glass case at 5pm for ₹20. It carries enormous nostalgia and zero prestige. Nobody has made the premium version a national brand.

An all-butter, properly laminated, hot-from-the-oven puff at ₹60 needs no education. People already know what a puff is; they've just never had a good one. That is the cheapest category position available to you, and it happens to be the exact thing your production line is built to make.

Coffee is not the wedge. Coffee is the economics: 80%+ gross margin, and the reason someone comes four mornings a week instead of once a fortnight. Pastry brings them in; coffee is why they stay and why the unit works. So: famous for puffs, profitable on coffee.

Who you're actually for

Primary

The 9:40 commuter

26–38, works in Indiranagar, Domlur or Old Airport Road. Buys a puff and a cortado three or four mornings a week. Spends ₹190–260. Wants a breakfast better than the office pantry and faster than a sit-down café.

Win them with: being open and fully baked by 7:30am, a two-minute queue, and a bag they can carry into a meeting.

Secondary

The Saturday household

Families in New Tippasandra, Jeevan Bhima Nagar and HAL — old cantonment residents and young renters both. Buys a ₹700–1,200 box on weekends, plus cakes for birthdays and Christmas.

Win them with: a weekend-only box, pre-order on WhatsApp, and being the neighbourhood's default celebration cake.

B2B

The café owner without a pastry chef

30–120 seat specialty cafés, cloud kitchens, corporate pantry vendors. Needs consistent laminated goods, daily or frozen, at a price that survives a 3× markup.

Win them with: consistency, 6am delivery windows, and a frozen SKU that lets them bake to demand.

The vibe

Don't do the Parisian bakery. It's been done on every second street in Indiranagar and it makes you compete on a story you can't own. Do the Bangalore cantonment bakery instead — which is what New Tippasandra actually is, and which nobody has made beautiful yet.

Red oxide floor. Kadappa stone counter. Steel bakery trays as display, not props. Butter paper and twine. Hand-painted signage in the old cantonment lettering. Bench seating for eight, standing room for more. Racks visible from the street so people see the trays come out hot. The whole brief is: a 1950s Bangalore bakery run by someone who takes lamination and espresso extraction seriously in 2026.

On the name

"Butterscotch Bakes" reads as a 1990s cake shop — pineapple cream, cherries on top, ₹450 birthday cakes. That's a real category, but it's not the one you're describing, and it will quietly cap what you can charge for a ₹185 almond croissant. Three options:

  • Keep and reframe. Cheapest. Lean into the retro cantonment reading and let the product do the repositioning. Works if your existing B2B accounts and neighbourhood already know you.
  • Endorse. "Butterscotch — Puff & Coffee." Keeps equity, adds the category. Low risk, moderate payoff.
  • Rename now. Most expensive and most correct if you're serious about goal 3. Do it before the flagship opens, not after — renaming a known café costs you a year of word of mouth.

My view: decide this before you spend on the flagship's signage, and don't decide it by asking friends which name they like. Ask which name makes a ₹185 pastry feel fair.

The first ninety days

Days 1–30 — Stop the bleeding, learn the truth

  • Build contribution margin by site. Sales minus COGS minus that site's rent, staff, power. Three numbers. This is the most valuable thing you'll do all quarter.
  • Decide the flagship. Best of the three on footfall, frontage and lease terms — not on sentiment.
  • Exit or convert the other two. Convert means: no seating, one staff, pickup and pre-order only, running as a satellite of the kitchen.
  • Reprice B2B. Most small bakeries are 8–15% underpriced on wholesale because they quoted before butter went up. Raise it; you'll lose fewer accounts than you fear.
  • Start logging wastage daily by SKU. You cannot fix what you don't weigh.
  • List every specialty café, cloud kitchen and office pantry vendor within 5km. Target 60 names.

Days 31–60 — Build the two ends

  • Sample-box campaign: 40 cold visits, one box each, one follow-up in 72 hours. Target 8 new accounts.
  • Quote the frozen SKU. Get a blast freezer costed and financed. Trial par-baked puffs and croissants with three friendly accounts.
  • Espresso setup at the flagship: machine, grinder, one trained barista, one roaster partnership. Open at 7:30am from day one.
  • Fix the product hierarchy. Two puffs, three viennoiserie, one danish of the week, four coffees. Cut the rest — a long menu at low volume is a wastage machine.
  • Start the corporate pitch: pantry supply for three offices within 3km, billed monthly in advance.

Days 61–90 — Make it repeatable

  • Hire the one person who unblocks you — a B2B salesperson if you'd rather run the café, a café manager if you'd rather sell. Not both, and not neither.
  • Write the recipe and production SOPs. Lamination is a process, and processes are what let you open a second site.
  • Turn on delivery for boxes and cakes only. ₹450 minimum. No single pastries, ever.
  • Run the numbers weekly, not monthly. Contribution margin, wastage %, coffee attach rate, top-3 B2B concentration, days sales outstanding.
  • Photograph the product properly, once. Your Instagram is your entire top-of-funnel for both the café and the wholesale pitch.

On the seven-year goal

Venture funding is a poor fit for a bakery chain and an excellent fit for a food manufacturing business with a consumer brand attached. If goal 3 is real, the frozen dough line is the part that makes you fundable — it's a factory with gross margins, contracts and geographic reach. The café chain is what makes it valuable and what makes it worth running. Build both now and you get to choose later; build only the cafés and the choice gets made for you.

Ten numbers I need before the next conversation

Everything above is a hypothesis built on ranges. Send these and I'll replace the ranges with your actuals, and the recommendation may well change.

  • Revenue split, last three months: B2B versus each of the three shops.
  • Rent, staff cost and lease end date for each shop.
  • Gross margin on your top ten SKUs — or at least food cost as a percentage of sales.
  • Number of active B2B accounts and what your largest three contribute.
  • Average payment days from your B2B customers.
  • Daily production volume and estimated unsold percentage.
  • Kitchen capacity: what could you produce tomorrow with the same equipment and one more shift?
  • Cash in the business, and what you personally need to draw each month.
  • Whether you own a sheeter, a retarder-proofer, and any freezer capacity.
  • How many hours a week you can give to selling, honestly.

If you only do one thing from this note: work out the contribution margin of each of the three shops this week. That number decides everything else — and it's the number nobody in a struggling three-site business wants to look at.
